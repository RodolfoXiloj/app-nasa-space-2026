## Project Name

Milpa Resiliente

## High-Level Project Summary

Milpa Resiliente turns two decades of satellite records into a three-fact traffic light designed for a smallholder farmer in Guatemala's Dry Corridor to read in seconds (the comprehension test with farmers is still pending): how many months a year the land around the plot shows bare soil or little plant cover (NASA MODIS NDVI, 250 m), how many years a satellite detected fire near the plot (NASA FIRMS VIIRS, 375 m; small or short fires can be missed), and how many years the mid-summer dry spell, the *canícula*, ran long (CHIRPS v3 daily rain, split into days with NASA GPM IMERG). Each fact has a shape, a word, a pictogram and a short audio in Spanish or English with subtitles in the same language, so it does not depend on colour or on reading well. One tap away, an Evidence view lets the extension worker compare three crop rotations per plot under drier and hotter scenarios and under the 2041-2060 climate that each of NASA's NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 models projects for that plot, with sources, validation and limits in plain sight. At the five plots, today's maize and beans keeps today's water score in only 2 to 9 of 33 or 34 NASA futures, while the drought-tolerant option holds in 32 to 34. It matters because drought keeps taking these families' maize and beans: for July-September 2026 Guatemala's food security agency forecast 3.2 million people (17 %) in crisis or worse (IPC Phase 3+), with Chiquimula and Zacapa at 15-19 %; FEWS NET reported 2026 crop losses for subsistence farmers, in some cases total; and in 2019 Oxfam measured average maize losses of 78.1 % in 201 monitored communities, 23 of them in our four Chiquimula municipalities. The tool does not decide for anyone: it gives the farmer and the extension worker the same evidence to decide together.

## Detailed Project Description

**What it does.** Milpa Resiliente is a static website for five example plots in Chiquimula and Zacapa (Jocotán, Camotán, Olopa, San Juan Ermita and Zacapa). It has two views and works in English and Spanish.

- **Farmer view (first screen).** Three facts the satellite saw around the plot, always in the same order: bare soil, fire near the plot and long canícula. Each fact gets one of four states: green circle “Good”, yellow triangle “Watch”, red square “Repeats”, or gray “The satellite can't see well here” when a source has no data. The view avoids orange and the word “alert”, so it is not mistaken for CONRED's public alert levels (green, yellow, orange and red); every state also carries a shape and a word, so it never relies on colour. Pictograms show the quantities: a 12-month wheel for bare soil and one dot per year for fire and dry spells. Below, the options to talk over with the extension worker: the current practice, the best legume or cover-crop option and the best drought-tolerant option. Options within 3 points of one another are shown as even, in no order, instead of naming a winner; the rule works as a chain, so in Olopa (87, 90 and 92) all three count as even. A Listen button plays a short synthetic-voice audio with subtitles, in Spanish or English to match the screen.
- **Evidence view (one tap away).** For the extension worker: a map with NASA GIBS true-colour layers stored in the site for offline use, plus Sentinel-2 imagery when there is a connection, what the satellite sees, the climate of the area, the three rotations side by side with water, soil and pest scores and a 12-month calendar, rainfall scenarios of 0, −10, −20 and −30 % with +1.5 °C, the three rotations tested in each NASA climate future for 2041-2060 (one dot per model), a printable farmer card, a “NASA product → decision” table, a “Where it fails” panel, the validation and every source.

**How it works.** A Python pipeline downloads and caches the data for each plot, recording its URL, date, product and version. A rules engine in plain Python (standard library plus PyYAML) detects bare-soil months, fires and dry spells, runs an FAO-56 water balance with yield response factors, scores the rotations and writes one JSON file per plot that follows a JSON schema. The web (React, Leaflet, Recharts, built with Vite) reads only those files, so it needs no server and no keys. Texts in both languages come from deterministic templates, and a validator rejects any number that is not in the engine's JSON and any planting date that does not come from ZARC (FAO–MAGA). Planting dates are quoted, never calculated.

**Local roots.** The cover and no-burn option follows the principles of Kuxur Rum (“my humid land” in Ch'orti'), an agroforestry system that FAO and Guatemala's Ministry of Agriculture have promoted in Chiquimula since 2000: permanent soil cover, no burning and more soil moisture through the canícula, the same three things the traffic light tracks. By 2006 more than 7,000 farms had adopted it. Jocotán, Camotán and Olopa are Ch'orti' Maya territory, and 112,432 people speak Ch'orti'. The data contract already reserves an audio slot for Ch'orti', and the repository includes the phrase script ready for native speakers to translate and record (`docs/chorti.md`). We did not machine-translate or synthesise Ch'orti': without review by speakers it could be wrong and disrespectful.

**How we test the rotations against the future.** In Google Earth Engine we took NASA's NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 daily projections (0.25°, bias-corrected) for each plot: May-October rain, June-August rain (the canícula) and mean temperature, for 2041-2060 against 1995-2014, in every model available (33 under SSP2-4.5 and 34 under SSP5-8.5). Each model is compared only with its own past, so its bias cancels out. The engine applies each model's change to the observed daily series and reruns the same water balance for the three rotations. A rotation "holds" in a future when its water score is equal to or higher than today's practice with today's climate. Earth Engine is used only offline to precompute the cache; the website never calls it. The median model projects 4 % less May-October rain, 7 % less June-August rain and +1.4 °C under SSP2-4.5, and 12 % less, 17 % less and +2.1 °C under SSP5-8.5. 23 to 24 of 33 models (SSP2-4.5) and 29 to 30 of 34 (SSP5-8.5) project less rain than today.

**How we checked it.** We fixed the rule before looking at the result: no threshold is tuned to make 2018 come out right. Using only data up to 2017 (water deficit with CHIRPS rain; SMAP is not part of this run), the engine flags 2018 as high risk at 5 of 5 plots. FAO GIEWS relayed a preliminary MAGA estimate of 175,126 ha of crop losses, mainly maize and beans, in Guatemala as of 13 August 2018. One year and five points are a sanity check, not a skill score, so we also count the misses. Over the 20 test years (2006-2025) the same rule flags 5 or 6 years per plot. Against the four dry years we had listed (2014, 2015, 2018, 2019) it hits 3, with 2 or 3 false alarms and 1 miss. Against seven years with publicly documented crop losses (2009, 2010, 2015, 2016, 2018, 2019, 2023) it hits 3 or 4 and misses 2016, 2019 and 2023 at every plot. We also measured where NASA POWER's default rainfall (about 50 km) fails at this scale (from −22 % to +55 % in July-August against CHIRPS) and used CHIRPS for rain instead; POWER now also offers IMERG rainfall at 0.1°, which we have not tested. Separately, the site shows a preliminary observation, not a forecast: in CHIRPS v3, the five plots' cells got 20 % to 26 % of their 1991-2025 median July-August rainfall in 2026, the lowest since 1991. FEWS NET reported 5 % to 25 % of normal August rainfall across the Dry Corridor, but its map uses the same Climate Hazards Center data, so it is consistent rather than independent.

**Benefits.** The farmer sees what satellites recorded around their own plot, in a form designed to work without reading well and to print in black and white; a real photocopy and a test with users are still pending. The extension worker gets the evidence, the confidence level and its reason for each option. The site is built to keep working without internet: an automated test with the network blocked passes, but it has not been tried in the field yet. Every claim links to its source and its limits.

**Limits we state openly.** The five points were chosen from NDVI and Sentinel-2 imagery and have not been checked on the ground. A Dry Corridor family farms about 0.35 ha on average, while one MODIS pixel covers 6.25 ha, a SMAP cell 9 km and an SRTM cell 90 m: the tool describes the land around the plot, not the plot. An agronomist confirmed three facts we rely on (ICTA B-7 is the most used maize in eastern Guatemala because of its drought tolerance, postrera beans are planted in August and September, and 10 days without rain at flowering already damage maize), but has not reviewed the scoring rules yet, and the traffic-light cut-offs were set after seeing the five plots' counts. The climate futures change mean rain and temperature, not the number of rainy days; Jocotán, Camotán and San Juan Ermita share one 0.25° NASA cell, so they share the projected change; and the drought-tolerant option starts with a higher water score, which favours it in the count. The pictograms still need a comprehension test with users. Camotán has no SMAP data of recommended quality. Suomi NPP stopped delivering new data in early November 2026 and Terra is being retired, so updates would move to VIIRS on NOAA-20 and NOAA-21. This is not an early-warning system and it does not replace ZARC, MAGA, INSIVUMEH or CONRED: it complements ZARC and the Agroclimatic Technical Boards (`docs/existing-tools.md`).

**Tools.** Python 3.11, PyYAML, requests, pytest and jsonschema; Google Earth Engine Code Editor (offline precompute only); JavaScript with React, Leaflet (react-leaflet), Recharts and Vite; edge-tts for the voice; a Windows laptop. No paid services.

## Use of Super Intelligence (SI)

An AI coding agent (Claude, by Anthropic) wrote most of the code, most of the texts in both languages and the SVG code of the pictograms, always under the team's direction and review. The same agent wrote the Earth Engine script and the module that tests the rotations in each NASA climate future. A research agent with web search found the agronomic sources and, on 14 November, the impact figures and the status of each sensor; every figure we use carries its URL, we checked every quote against its document, and the ones that could not be confirmed were left out. Before building, an AI assistant compared this idea with five others; the team chose. The team decided the scope, the five plots, the traffic-light thresholds, the tie rule and the validation rule. The audio uses synthetic voices (edge-tts: es-GT-MartaNeural and en-US-JennyNeural), labelled as AI-generated next to the player and in each mp3's tags. No AI produces any number the user sees: numbers come from the data and from rules in code, and texts come from fixed templates checked by a validator that rejects any number that is not in the engine's output. Full declaration: `docs/ai-use.md` in the repository.

## NASA Data

- **MODIS MOD13Q1 NDVI v061, 250 m, every 16 days, through ORNL DAAC:** months of bare soil or low cover in the 250 m cell around each plot (the “soil” fact) and the NDVI signal in the 2018 test. Terra is being retired in late 2026/early 2027; the method transfers to VIIRS vegetation indices.
- **FIRMS VIIRS 375 m active fires (Suomi NPP, 2012-2026):** years with at least one detection within 500 m of each plot between January and May (the “fire” fact). Each detection is the centre of a ~375 m pixel, and small or short fires can be missed. S-NPP stopped delivering new data in early November 2026; updates would use VIIRS on NOAA-20 and NOAA-21.
- **GPM IMERG Late V07 (0.1°):** used through CHIRPS v3 daily “sat”, which splits each CHIRPS pentad into days with IMERG's daily pattern; we did not download IMERG directly. Dry-spell counts depend on this split, so we compared them with the ERA5-based “rnl” version: with “rnl” the 5 plots have 14 years with a dry spell of more than 15 days in 2001-2025 against 8 with “sat” (plot-years), the two agree on 7 of the 15 plot-years either one flags, and the traffic-light colour changes in 3 of 5 plots (Jocotán yellow→red, Olopa green→yellow, San Juan Ermita yellow→red).
- **NASA POWER (about 50 km):** temperature and reference evapotranspiration for the water balance, and the daily pattern used to split monthly CHIRPS rain outside June-August; also measured against CHIRPS to show where it fails.
- **SMAP SPL3SMP_E v6 (9 km grid, daily, top 0-5 cm of soil), through AppEEARS:** a separate soil-moisture check next to the 2018 test (2018 against the other SMAP years, 2015-2026; it is not part of the “data up to 2017” run), the 2026 observation and the confidence level. It describes the surrounding ~9 km landscape, not the plot.
- **SRTM GL3 (90 m):** slope of the 90 m cell that contains each plot.
- **NASA GIBS:** true-colour MODIS layer stored in the site for the offline map.
- **NEX-GDDP-CMIP6, 0.25°, through Google Earth Engine (precomputed):** the change in rain and temperature that each climate model projects for 2041-2060 at each plot, used to test the three rotations in 33 (SSP2-4.5) and 34 (SSP5-8.5) futures. Thrasher et al. (2022), Scientific Data 9, 262, doi:10.1038/s41597-022-01393-4.
